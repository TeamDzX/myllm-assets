The first duty of a government, countless leaders over the decades have said, is to safeguard its population and to do so without fear or favour.

On 11 September 2001 the United States manifestly failed to do that, and the consequences reshaped a generation of policy.

The intelligence was there, scattered across agencies that did not speak to one another, yet nobody joined up the dots in time.

What changed afterwards

Budgets tripled, agencies were merged, and a whole architecture of surveillance was built in under three years.

Whether any of it would have stopped the plot itself remains the question nobody in the building likes being asked.

Two decades on, the same structural problem recurs in a different shape, with different agencies failing to share.

  • Budgets tripled within three years of the attacks
  • Sixteen agencies were folded into one department

The lesson, if there is one, is that collection was never the constraint — analysis and the willingness to act on it were.
