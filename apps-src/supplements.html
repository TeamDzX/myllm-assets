How to read this

Each entry says what an ingredient is, how good the evidence is for what people take it for, typical amounts, cautions, and its legal status.

Good means several solid human trials agree. Some means small or mixed trials. Little means mostly lab, animal or traditional use.

The rules, briefly

US. Under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994 (DSHEA), supplements don't need FDA approval before sale. Makers must make sure they're safe and labelled truthfully, and FDA acts after problems appear. A label may say how a product affects the body, but not that it treats a disease. Ingredients sold after October 1994 need a new dietary ingredient notification, and anything first approved or studied as a drug generally can't be sold as a supplement.

UK and EU. Supplements are regulated as food. Ingredients not eaten widely before May 1997 need novel food authorisation. Herbal products making medicinal claims need a medicines licence or a Traditional Herbal Registration (THR) from the MHRA.

Sport. Athletes are responsible for anything in their body. The WADA list applies whatever the label says.

Where the live data comes from

Molecules are drawn from PubChem (US National Library of Medicine). Reports to FDA come from openFDA's CAERS database: people and companies report problems after taking a product, which does not prove the product caused them. Summaries come from Wikipedia.
